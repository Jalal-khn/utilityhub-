Body Mass Index, or BMI, is one of the most widely used screening tools for assessing weight relative to height. It is quick to calculate, requires only two measurements, and gives a general indication of whether someone falls into an underweight, healthy, overweight, or obesity category.

This guide covers **how BMI is calculated**, what the numbers mean, the formula behind them, common mistakes, and important limitations you should understand before drawing conclusions from a BMI number.

## What is BMI?

BMI is a number calculated from your weight and height. It was developed in the 1830s by a Belgian mathematician named Adolphe Quetelet, and it remains a standard screening tool used by doctors, researchers, and health organizations worldwide.

The number itself is simple: weight divided by height squared. A person who weighs 70 kilograms and is 1.75 meters tall has a BMI of 22.86. That number is then compared to standard categories to determine a general weight range.

BMI is useful because it provides a quick, standardized way to compare weight across different heights. Two people who weigh the same but have different heights will have different BMIs, which gives a fairer picture than weight alone.

## How is BMI calculated?

The standard metric formula is:

**BMI = weight (kg) / height (m)²**

In other words, divide your weight in kilograms by the square of your height in meters.

For those using imperial units (pounds and inches), the formula is:

**BMI = 703 × weight (lb) / height (in)²**

The 703 conversion factor adjusts for the difference between metric and imperial units.

<InfoBox type="tip" title="Square the height, not the weight">
The most common mistake is squaring the wrong number. BMI divides weight by height squared, not the other way around. Double-check the formula before calculating.
</InfoBox>

## BMI calculation example

Let us walk through a clear example using the metric formula.

**Given:**
- Weight: 70 kg
- Height: 1.75 m

**Step 1: Square the height**

1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625

**Step 2: Divide weight by the squared height**

70 / 3.0625 = 22.86

**BMI = 22.86**

This falls in the healthy weight range (18.5 to 24.9).

Here is the same calculation in imperial units:

**Given:**
- Weight: 154 lb
- Height: 5 feet 9 inches = 69 inches

**Step 1: Square the height**

69 × 69 = 4,761

**Step 2: Multiply 703 by weight, then divide by squared height**

(703 × 154) / 4,761 = 108,262 / 4,761 = 22.74

The slight difference (22.86 vs 22.74) comes from the conversion factor rounding, but both results fall in the same category.

## BMI categories

The World Health Organization classifies adult BMI into the following ranges:

| BMI range | Category |
| --- | --- |
| Below 18.5 | Underweight |
| 18.5 - 24.9 | Healthy weight |
| 25.0 - 29.9 | Overweight |
| 30.0 - 34.9 | Obesity (Class I) |
| 35.0 - 39.9 | Obesity (Class II) |
| 40.0 and above | Obesity (Class III) |

These categories are general screening ranges. They are not a diagnosis and do not replace professional medical assessment.

<InfoBox type="warning" title="BMI categories are general guidelines">
BMI categories apply broadly to adult populations but may not accurately reflect the health status of every individual. Athletes, older adults, and people with certain body compositions may have a BMI that does not correspond to their actual health.
</InfoBox>

## What does a BMI number mean?

A BMI number tells you where your weight falls relative to your height according to the standard categories:

- **Below 18.5** - weight is lower than what is generally considered healthy for your height
- **18.5 to 24.9** - weight is within the range typically considered healthy
- **25.0 to 29.9** - weight is higher than the healthy range for your height
- **30.0 and above** - weight is significantly above the healthy range

These are general indicators. BMI does not directly measure body fat, and it does not account for factors like muscle mass, bone density, age, sex, or overall health context.

## BMI limitations

BMI is a screening tool, not a complete health assessment. It has several important limitations:

**Muscle mass** - muscle is denser than fat. A muscular person may have a high BMI despite having low body fat. This is why athletes often have BMIs in the overweight or obesity range while being in excellent physical condition.

**Body composition** - BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone. Two people with identical BMIs can have very different amounts of body fat.

**Age** - BMI ranges for older adults may differ from those for younger adults. Body composition changes with age in ways that BMI does not capture.

**Sex** - men and women typically have different body fat distributions and muscle mass. BMI categories are the same for both, which limits accuracy for individual assessment.

**Pregnancy** - BMI is not meaningful during pregnancy, as weight changes are expected and necessary.

**Individual health context** - BMI does not account for blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, fitness level, or other factors that contribute to overall health.

For a complete picture of health, BMI should be considered alongside other assessments and professional medical guidance.

<InfoBox type="info" title="BMI is a starting point, not a conclusion">
BMI provides a quick snapshot of weight relative to height. It is useful for population-level screening and general awareness, but it should not be used alone to make decisions about health or treatment.
</InfoBox>

Notice how the same weight produces different BMIs at different heights. A person weighing 80 kg has a BMI of 26.12 at 1.75 m (overweight) but 24.69 at 1.80 m (healthy weight). This is exactly why BMI uses both weight and height.

## Common BMI calculation mistakes

- **Using centimeters instead of meters** - BMI requires height in meters, not centimeters. A height of 175 cm should be entered as 1.75 m. Using 175 in the formula produces a wildly incorrect result.
- **Confusing pounds and kilograms** - the metric formula requires kilograms. If your weight is in pounds, divide by 2.205 first.
- **Forgetting to square the height** - BMI divides weight by height squared. If you divide by height without squaring, the number is meaningless.
- **Entering incorrect height** - even a small error in height changes the BMI noticeably. Double-check your measurement.
- **Rounding too early** - round your BMI only at the final step. Rounding intermediate values introduces unnecessary error.

BMI (Body Mass Index) is a number calculated from your weight and height. It is a screening tool that categorizes weight relative to height into ranges such as underweight, healthy weight, overweight, and obesity.

### How do I calculate BMI?
Divide your weight in kilograms by the square of your height in meters. For example, 70 kg divided by (1.75 m × 1.75 m) equals 22.86.

### What is the BMI formula?
The metric formula is BMI = weight (kg) / height (m)². The imperial formula is BMI = 703 × weight (lb) / height (in)².

For adults, a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is generally considered a healthy weight range. This is a general guideline, not a diagnostic standard.
