The gap between the salary you accepted and the number that lands in your bank account surprises almost everyone starting their first job. A "$60,000 salary" does not pay $5,000 monthly - and understanding exactly where the missing money goes is the first step toward legally keeping more of it.

This guide explains **gross versus net pay**, demystifies progressive tax brackets (including the myth that stops people from accepting raises), and walks through real numbers for the US, UK, and Canada.

## The vocabulary that matters

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gross pay | Your full salary before anything is deducted |
| Net pay | What actually reaches your bank ("take-home") |
| Withholding | Estimated tax your employer sends to the government per paycheck |
| Marginal rate | Tax rate on your *next* dollar |
| Effective rate | Total tax / total income - your true average |

## How progressive brackets really work

The most persistent money myth: "a raise pushing me into a higher bracket means I take home less." Completely false.

Brackets work like filled buckets - only income inside each bucket gets that bucket's rate. US federal brackets for a single filer:

| Bracket | Rate | Income range |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10% | $0 – $11,925 |
| 2 | 12% | $11,926 – $48,475 |
| 3 | 22% | $48,476 – $103,350 |
| 4 | 24% | $103,351 – $197,300 |

Earning $50,000 does not tax all $50,000 at 22%. Only the dollars above $48,475 touch that rate.

## Worked example: $60,000 in the United States

Single filer, standard deduction ($15,000):

1. Taxable income: $60,000 - $15,000 = **$45,000**
2. First $11,925 taxed at 10% = $1,193
3. Remaining $33,075 taxed at 12% = $3,969
4. Federal income tax ≈ **$5,162**
5. Effective federal rate: 8.6% - despite a "12% bracket"

Then Social Security (6.2% up to the wage cap) and Medicare (1.45%, no cap) add roughly $4,590. Net before state tax and elections: approximately **$50,250**, or ~$4,190 monthly.

## Worked example: £40,000 in the United Kingdom

1. Personal allowance: first £12,570 = **£0 tax**
2. Basic rate: (£40,000 - £12,570) x 20% = **£5,486**
3. National Insurance (8% between £12,570 and £50,270): ≈ **£2,195**
4. Take-home ≈ **£32,320**, or about £2,693 monthly

## Worked example: C$70,000 in Canada

1. Basic personal amount (~$16,129): **C$0 tax** on that portion
2. 15% on the next ~$41,246 = **$6,187**
3. 20.5% on remaining ~$12,625 = **$2,588**
4. Federal tax ≈ **$8,775**, plus CPP/EI ≈ **$4,300**
5. Provincial tax varies by province; Ontario adds roughly $3,900 here
6. Approximate take-home: **~$53,000**, or ~$4,420 monthly

## Why your effective rate beats your bracket

Notice every example: the person "in the 22% bracket" pays an effective 11%; the "20% taxpayer" keeps most of their money. Deductions, allowances, and low-bracket fill-up mean **effective rates typically run half or less of top marginal rates** for middle incomes.

## The legitimate ways to keep more

1. **Pre-tax retirement accounts** (401k, pension, RRSP): contributions dodge income tax now
2. **HSAs/FSAs**: medical and childcare spending with pre-tax money
3. **Claim every deduction**: student loan interest, eligible expenses, credits
4. **Fix your W-4/tax code**: over-withholding is an interest-free loan to the government
5. **Benefits packages**: paying premiums pre-tax raises net pay slightly

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a raise ever reduce take-home pay?**
Through genuine tax mechanics, no. Edge cases exist around benefit cliffs and repayment thresholds, but bracket jumps themselves always increase net income.

**Why did my paycheck shrink mid-year?**
Common causes: hitting the Social Security wage cap (paycheck grows), benefit premium increases, corrected withholding, or bonus payments taxed at supplemental rates.

**Are bonuses taxed at 40%?**
Bonuses use flat supplemental withholding (22% federal in the US), which can feel high if your overall bracket is lower - but you reclaim any excess when filing your return.

**What is the Social Security wage base?**
The maximum earnings subject to the 6.2% Social Security tax, adjusted yearly. Income above it escapes SS tax but remains subject to Medicare.

**Do I get tax-free allowance if I have two jobs?**
Allowances typically apply fully only to one income. Second jobs usually face higher effective withholding - plan for it rather than being surprised.

**How accurate are online take-home calculators?**
For standard employment with typical deductions, very close - usually within a small margin of payroll software results. Complex situations (multiple incomes, unusual benefits) need professional software or advice.
